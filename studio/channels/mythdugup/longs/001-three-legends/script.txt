For hundreds of years, experts said these places were just stories. Then someone picked up a shovel. Today, we're digging up three legends that turned out to be real.
# Troy
Let's start with the most famous war that supposedly never happened. Homer's Iliad tells of Troy, a rich city on the coast of what is now Turkey. A ten-year siege. Heroes. And a giant wooden horse full of soldiers.
For most of history, people loved the story but didn't believe it. Troy was poetry, not geography. Scholars filed it next to the Cyclops and the gods on Mount Olympus.
Then came Heinrich Schliemann, a German businessman who had made a fortune and wanted to prove Homer right. In eighteen seventy-one, he started digging into a hill called Hisarlik. And he didn't dig carefully. He cut a huge trench straight through it.
What he found was not one city. It was layer after layer of cities, built on top of each other. Archaeologists now count nine main layers, from about three thousand years before Christ all the way to Roman times.
So was it Troy? Today, most archaeologists agree that Hisarlik is the site of Troy. One of the middle layers, often called Troy six or seven, matches the time of the legend. But the war itself, the horse, the heroes? That's still debated. The city was real. The story around it is a different question.
# Pompeii
Our second legend is a city that was famous for being lost. In seventy-nine A.D., Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried Pompeii under ash. The Romans wrote about it. And then, over the centuries, people simply forgot where it was.
Farmers grew vines on top of it. A canal was even dug right through it, without anyone noticing what lay below.
In seventeen forty-eight, excavators started digging, and Pompeii came back. Streets. Shops. Bakeries. Paintings on the walls. A mosaic of a dog by a front door, with the words cave canem. Beware of the dog.
The people were gone, but they had left something behind. Their bodies had decayed inside the hardened ash, leaving hollow spaces in the exact shape of their last moments. In eighteen sixty-three, Giuseppe Fiorelli had an idea. He poured plaster into those hollows. And the citizens of Pompeii appeared again.
For a long time, everyone knew the date of the eruption: the twenty-fourth of August. But in twenty eighteen, archaeologists found a charcoal inscription on a wall that seems to point to October instead. So the city is proven. The exact day is still debated.
# Vinland
Our last legend sailed across an ocean. Icelandic sagas tell of Leif Erikson, a Norse explorer who sailed west from Greenland and found a land full of grapes and forests. They called it Vinland.
If that was true, Vikings had reached America almost five hundred years before Columbus. For centuries, it sounded like a nice story and nothing more.
Then, in nineteen sixty, a Norwegian couple, Helge Ingstad and Anne Stine Ingstad, followed the clues to the northern tip of Newfoundland, in Canada. Locals showed them strange bumps in the grass.
Under those bumps were the remains of Norse buildings, made of turf. They found iron rivets, and a small stone spindle whorl, used for spinning wool. A real Viking camp.
And in twenty twenty-one, scientists went one step further. A burst of radiation from the sun left a marker in tree rings in the year nine ninety-three. By counting the rings in wood that had been cut with metal tools, they dated the cutting to one exact year: ten twenty-one.
So the Vikings were really there. Where exactly the Vinland of the sagas was, though, is still debated.
