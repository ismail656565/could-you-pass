In nineteen sixty-three, a seventeen-year-old decided not to sleep. Not for one night. For eleven days. This is a science story, not a challenge. Please don't try it.
His name was Randy Gardner. His family had just moved to San Diego, and he wanted to win the city science fair.
The record for staying awake was two hundred and sixty hours, held by a radio DJ in Honolulu. Randy wanted to beat it.
He recruited two friends from school, Bruce McAllister and Joe Marciano. Their job was to keep him awake, around the clock, and write down everything that happened.
It began on the twenty-eighth of December. The first day was easy. By the second day, simple tongue twisters became hard.
Three days in, Randy felt nauseous and had trouble remembering things. His mood swung up and down.
Then the story reached Stanford. A young sleep researcher named William Dement drove to San Diego to watch. He was one of the very few people in the world who studied sleep at the time.
A navy medic, John Ross, kept track of Randy's health. Dement kept him moving, with basketball and games of pinball.
As the days went on, Randy's thinking slowed down. He struggled with simple math, and at times he seemed to see things that were not there.
# The Record
On the eighth of January, nineteen sixty-four, it was over. Randy had been awake for two hundred and sixty-four hours. Eleven days and about twenty-five minutes.
Then he slept. For just over fourteen hours. When he woke up, he said he felt groggy, but no groggier than a normal person.
The three friends won first place at the science fair. The record went into the Guinness book.
But years later, Guinness stopped accepting sleep records at all, because staying awake that long is dangerous. And Randy himself later said he struggled with insomnia as an adult.
