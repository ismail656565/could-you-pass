In nineteen forty-four, the United States sent a secret unit to war. Its weapons were rubber tanks, loudspeakers, and paint brushes. And for more than fifty years, almost nobody was allowed to talk about it.
Its official name was the twenty-third Headquarters Special Troops. The soldiers called it something else. The Ghost Army.
It was activated in January nineteen forty-four, at Camp Forrest in Tennessee. About eleven hundred men. And many of them were not typical soldiers at all.
The army had gone looking for artists. Painters, designers, sculptors, photographers. People who knew how to make something look real.
Among them was a young man named Bill Blass, who later became a famous fashion designer. And a painter named Ellsworth Kelly, who later became one of America's best known abstract artists.
# The Tricks
Their job was simple to describe and very hard to do. Make the enemy see an army where there was none.
First, the eyes. The unit carried inflatable tanks, trucks and artillery made of rubber. Pumped up at night, they looked real from the air and from a distance.
Then, the ears. Sound engineers recorded real units: engines, tracks, men building bridges. Huge speakers on trucks played those sounds into the night, so the enemy could hear an army moving.
And finally, the radio. Operators sent fake messages, copying the style of real units, so the enemy listening in would hear a whole division that wasn't there.
They even acted. They sewed on fake patches, painted fake markings on trucks, and told stories in cafes, knowing spies might be listening.
After landing in France in the summer of nineteen forty-four, the Ghost Army staged about twenty deceptions, in France, Luxembourg, Belgium and Germany.
Their biggest show came in March nineteen forty-five, near the Rhine river. It was called Operation Viersen. The Ghost Army pretended to be two entire infantry divisions, to draw attention away from the real crossing.
It worked. The real Ninth Army crossed the Rhine.
When the war ended, the men went home and said nothing. The missions stayed classified for decades. Much of the record was only declassified in nineteen ninety-six.
In twenty twenty-four, the surviving members were finally honored with the Congressional Gold Medal, the highest honor Congress can give.
